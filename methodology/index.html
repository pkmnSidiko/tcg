Field Methodology

How I Collect

My collection has rules. Some are practical, some are aesthetic, and some exist because I have apparently decided that cardboard requires a filing system.

There isn't one universal set of rules for every card I own. Different binders and collection projects have different purposes, and the rules follow the purpose.

This page documents the systems I use to decide where cards belong, what counts as complete, and how I handle the particularly complicated bits of Pokémon TCG collecting.

Binder Behavior

A binder is not simply a container for cards. Each binder has a job, and cards are assigned according to that job.

The Universal Rule

All cards stored in a binder are kept in a penny sleeve.

Foil cards receive an additional layer of protection: they are placed in a perfect-fit sleeve inside a penny sleeve.

The penny sleeve is the baseline protection for binder cards. Additional sleeve choices and colors are determined by the collection system.

Collection Binders

Collection binders follow the universal sleeve rules above. Cards are assigned to a binder according to the purpose and rules of that particular collection.

A card appearing in one binder does not automatically mean it belongs in another binder where it could technically fit. The collection is made up of overlapping systems rather than one master list.

Trade Binders

I maintain two trade binders, each with a different purpose.

The first is a singles trade binder. This contains individual cards being offered for trade and is organized as a curated selection rather than a bulk inventory.

The second is a duplicates binder for bulk trades. This is where duplicate cards that are available for trading are stored.

The duplicates binder holds up to four copies of the same card in a single binder slot.

If I have more than four copies of a particular card, that card may occupy additional binder slots rather than stacking more than four copies into one pocket.

Because of the quantity involved, bulk duplicates do not necessarily each have an individual penny sleeve while they are stored in the binder.

When a bulk duplicate is not already in a penny sleeve, a penny sleeve from a pack is kept available inside the binder so that the card can be sleeved when it is prepared for trade.

Foil & Reverse Holo Policy

For my collection purposes, foil is a physical classification. A card is considered foil if it is printed on holo stock or contains a holofoil lamination overlay.

This classification exists primarily because foil cards curl. Changes in temperature, humidity, storage conditions, and handling can all contribute to curling, making foil cards more difficult to store and maintain.

This is a preservation rule, not a statement about monetary value. A card does not need to be rare or expensive to deserve reasonable protection.

Foil Protection

Every card that meets the foil definition is placed in a perfect-fit sleeve inside a penny sleeve.

This applies to foil cards regardless of whether the card is considered a regular holo, reverse holo, or another type of foil treatment.

Foil status is primarily a handling and storage classification. It does not automatically determine which collection or binder a card belongs to.

Foil & Collection Placement

Some collection projects distinguish between regular and reverse-holo versions as separate cards. In those projects, the distinction matters for completeness.

In other projects, foil status can be an aesthetic choice. For example, a reverse-holo card may be selected for the Living Pokédex because its appearance works better with the surrounding cards.

The physical foil classification remains the same regardless of why the card was selected.

Foil Maintenance

Foil cards that develop troublesome curls may be carefully flattened or otherwise repaired as part of normal collection maintenance.

The goal is preservation: correcting the physical problem without damaging the card.

Foil handling practices are informed by decades of TCG experience, including collecting Pokémon cards since the beginning of the Pokémon TCG and playing Magic: The Gathering since 1997.

Sleeve System

Sleeve color is part of my organization system rather than simply an aesthetic choice.

The system is thematic rather than strictly categorical. A sleeve color provides a visual cue about the personality, subject, or purpose of a collection.

Master Sets & Collections

Sleeve colors for master-set-style collections are chosen according to the overall vibe of the collection.

The color may be inspired by the set's artwork, branding, subject matter, or simply the color that feels right for the collection.

Living Pokédex

Living Pokédex binders use standard penny sleeves rather than a thematic sleeve color.

Foil cards still follow the foil protection rule and therefore receive a perfect-fit sleeve inside the penny sleeve.

Pokémon Collections

Sleeve colors for Pokémon-specific collections are based on the dominant color associated with the Pokémon's Energy type.

This provides a visual connection between the Pokémon and the collection without requiring every Pokémon binder to use the same color.

Artist Collections

Artist collections use a color associated with the artist's favorite Pokémon.

For example, Yuka Morii's favorite Pokémon is Ditto, while Asako Ito's is Goomy. Both are associated with a light-purple sleeve color for their respective collections.

Special Collections

Special and themed collections receive sleeve colors based on the theme of the collection.

For example, Halloween collections may use orange, McDonald's collections may use red, and winter-themed collections may use an ice-blue color.

The color is intended to reinforce the theme rather than serve as a rigid database identifier.

What "Complete" Means

"Complete" does not mean the same thing for every collection.

A collection is complete when it has reached the defined boundaries of that particular project. Those boundaries are established by purpose rather than by a single universal checklist.

Pokémon Master Collections

A Pokémon master collection aims to include every qualifying English-language printing of the Pokémon within the defined scope of the project.

Different English printings count separately. This includes regular cards, holo and non-holo versions, reverse holos, promos, and other distinct English printings.

Special or event-distribution cards may be skipped. These are not required for a collection to be considered complete under my practical collecting rules.

Cameos do not count. The Pokémon must be the subject of the card rather than simply appearing incidentally in the artwork.

TAG TEAM cards are also excluded from Pokémon master collections. These cards have a deliberate home in the Michi Method instead.

Evolution-Line Collections

Evolution-line collections follow the same English-language printing rules as Pokémon master collections, but apply them to a defined evolutionary family.

The scope can include pre-evolutions, evolutions, and other members of the defined line according to the individual project.

Artist Master Collections

Artist collections focus on the artist's Pokémon TCG work as a body of art.

The current focus is English-language work, with every qualifying English printing collected where practical.

Distinct versions such as regular common or uncommon cards, rare holo and non-holo cards, reverse holos, promos, and other English printings are part of the collection.

These collections are particularly interested in artists whose work has a distinctive artistic medium or recognizable technique.

Living Pokédex

The Living Pokédex is not a complete-printing collection. Its purpose is to provide a representation of each Pokémon and its relevant evolutionary relationships.

The selected card can be replaced when a preferred artwork is found. A Living Pokédex entry is therefore upgradeable rather than permanently locked to the first qualifying card acquired.

Michi Method

Michi Method collections are aesthetic compositions rather than checklist projects.

A spread may be built around an anchor card, a Pokémon, an artist, a visual theme, or a particular idea.

Multiple printings of an anchor card may intentionally be included when they contribute to the composition.

For example, the current "Moonlit Nightmares" spread is built around Umbreon & Darkrai TAG TEAM cards, with both the regular and promo printings serving as anchor cards alongside supporting cards and illustrations.

A Michi Method spread is complete when its visual composition feels resolved. There is no requirement to own every possible card that could technically fit the theme.

Future Set Completion

Traditional set-completion projects remain a future goal.

These projects depend on being able to acquire sealed product at reasonable prices. Modern sets may become practical when they receive reprints or reissues, while vintage sets are approached through the secondary market rather than waiting for new vintage product to be produced.

Language & Variant Rules

Language is a project rule, not a measure of whether a card is collectible.

English Collection Focus

English-language cards are the focus of my Pokémon, evolution-line, and artist master collections.

The goal is not to collect every language in which a card was printed. Keeping these projects English-focused makes the scope manageable while still allowing the Living Pokédex and special collections to be more flexible.

Non-English Cards

Non-English cards are welcome in the Living Pokédex and in special or aesthetic collections.

A non-English card may be chosen because its artwork is preferred, because it is substantially easier to acquire, or because the card is significantly less expensive than its English counterpart.

A non-English card does not replace a qualifying English printing in an English-focused master collection.

Intentional Variants

Intentional card variants are part of the collection when they fall within the rules of the project.

This can include regular and holo versions, reverse holos, promos, special printings, and other deliberately produced variants.

Accidental manufacturing variations are not treated as required collection categories.

A particularly interesting printing quirk can certainly make a card more fun to own, but there is no requirement to hunt down every possible swirl, print variation, or other manufacturing oddity.

Illustration Rare & Special Illustration Rare

Illustration Rare (IR) and Special Illustration Rare (SIR) cards are valid parts of master collections.

They are specifically excluded from the Living Pokédex, where card selection follows the separate representation and aesthetic rules of that project.

Living Pokédex Rules

The Living Pokédex follows its own rules because its purpose is different from that of a master collection.

The goal is to represent Pokémon and their relationships in a coherent binder system rather than to collect every printing of every card.

Evolutionary Relationships

Pre-evolutions and evolutions remain together.

Branching evolutionary lines remain grouped together rather than being separated into unrelated Pokédex sections.

Eevee and its Eeveelutions are treated as one evolutionary group.

Regional Forms

Regional forms remain with the Pokémon family they belong to.

Regional variants and their associated additional evolutions are therefore kept together rather than separated solely according to their individual National Pokédex numbers.

The different Meowth regional forms and their additional evolutions are one example of this approach.

Forme Changes

Forme changes remain associated with their Pokémon rather than being treated as unrelated entries.

Card Selection

    ```
  • Language does not matter.
  • Illustration Rare (IR) and Special Illustration Rare (SIR) cards are excluded.
  • EX, GX, V, and VSTAR cards may be used.
  • Reverse-holo cards may be selected when their appearance is preferred.
  • Evolution lines use the same Pokémon TCG Energy type.
  • The selected card may be upgraded later when a preferred artwork is found.

The Living Pokédex Is Allowed to Change

The Living Pokédex is not considered permanently finished simply because every slot is occupied.

If a better artwork, preferred printing, or more aesthetically satisfying card is found, the existing representation can be upgraded.

The binder is intended to be a living collection, not a museum exhibit frozen at the moment each Pokémon was first acquired.

The Short Version

Different binder. Different rules. Different definition of complete.

Some cards are collected for completeness. Some are collected for representation. Some are collected because they make the page look ridiculously good together.

Same cardboard obsession.
